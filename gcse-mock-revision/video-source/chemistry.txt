@chapter 1. Key concepts: atoms
@scene CHEMISTRY | Key concepts | Inside an atom
@diagram atom
- Nucleus: **protons** (+1) and **neutrons** (0) >> Every atom has a tiny nucleus in the middle. It contains protons, with a charge of plus one, and neutrons, with no charge.
- Shells: **electrons** (−1) >> Electrons, with a charge of minus one, move around the nucleus in shells.
- Protons and neutrons: mass 1 · electrons: tiny >> Protons and neutrons both have a mass of one. Electrons are so light, their mass is almost nothing.
- Atoms have no overall charge >> An atom has no overall charge, because it has the same number of protons and electrons.

@scene CHEMISTRY | Key concepts | Atomic number and mass number
@box Sodium | mass number 23 | atomic number 11
- Atomic number = number of **protons** >> The atomic number, the smaller number, is the number of protons.
- Mass number = protons + neutrons >> The mass number, the bigger number, is protons plus neutrons.
- Sodium: 11 protons, 11 electrons >> Sodium has atomic number eleven. So it has eleven protons and eleven electrons.
- Neutrons = 23 − 11 = **12** >> Neutrons are mass number minus atomic number: twenty-three minus eleven is twelve.
- Isotopes: same protons, **different neutrons** >> Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons.

@scene CHEMISTRY | Key concepts | Electron shells and the periodic table
@box Fill shells | 2, then 8, then 8
- Sodium (11): **2.8.1** >> Fill the shells two, then eight, then eight. Sodium has eleven electrons: two, eight, one.
- Chlorine (17): **2.8.7** >> Chlorine has seventeen: two, eight, seven.
- Group = number of **outer electrons** >> The group number is the number of electrons in the outer shell. Sodium is in group one.
- Period = number of **shells** >> The period, or row, is the number of shells.
- Metals on the left, non-metals on the right >> Metals are on the left of the periodic table. Non-metals are on the right.
- Mendeleev left **gaps** and predicted properties >> Mendeleev left gaps for elements not yet discovered, and predicted their properties. He was later proved right.

@scene CHEMISTRY | Key concepts | Ions and bonding
@box Ions | metals LOSE electrons: + ions | non-metals GAIN: − ions
- Metals **lose** electrons → positive ions (Na⁺) >> An ion is a charged atom. Metals lose electrons to make positive ions.
- Non-metals **gain** electrons → negative ions (Cl⁻) >> Non-metals gain electrons to make negative ions. Both do this to get a full outer shell.
- Ionic: metal + non-metal, electrons **transferred** >> Ionic bonding is between a metal and a non-metal. Electrons are transferred, and the opposite charges attract strongly.
- High melting point · conducts when **molten or dissolved** >> Ionic compounds have high melting points. They conduct electricity when molten or dissolved, because the ions can move.
- Covalent: non-metals **share** pairs of electrons >> Covalent bonding is between non-metals. They share pairs of electrons.
- Simple molecules: **low** melting point >> Small covalent molecules have low melting points, because the forces between molecules are weak.

@scene CHEMISTRY | Key concepts | Formulas and balancing equations
@box Common ions | OH⁻ hydroxide | NO₃⁻ nitrate | CO₃²⁻ carbonate | SO₄²⁻ sulfate
- Charges must cancel: Mg²⁺ + Cl⁻ → **MgCl₂** >> In a formula, the charges must cancel out. Magnesium is two plus and chloride is one minus, so we need two chlorides: MgCl₂.
- Mg + O₂ → MgO ✗ >> Equations must balance: the same number of each atom on both sides.
- Mg + O₂ → **2**MgO >> There are two oxygens on the left, so put a two in front of MgO.
- **2**Mg + O₂ → 2MgO ✔ >> Now there are two magnesiums on the right, so put a two in front of Mg. Balanced.
- Only change the **big numbers** in front >> Only ever change the big numbers in front, never the small ones.

@scene CHEMISTRY | Key concepts | Relative formula mass and concentration
@box Concentration | g/dm³ = mass ÷ volume | 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³
- CO₂ = 12 + 16 + 16 = **44** >> To find the relative formula mass, add up the atomic masses. Carbon dioxide is twelve plus sixteen plus sixteen: forty-four.
- CaCO₃ = 40 + 12 + 48 = **100** >> Calcium carbonate is forty plus twelve plus three sixteens: one hundred.
- Mass seems to drop → a **gas escaped** >> Mass is always conserved. If the mass seems to go down, a gas escaped.
- 10 g in 250 cm³: 250 cm³ = 0.25 dm³ >> Concentration: ten grams dissolved in two hundred and fifty centimetres cubed. First, change to decimetres cubed: zero point two five.
- 10 ÷ 0.25 = **40 g/dm³** >> Ten divided by zero point two five is forty grams per decimetre cubed.

- **7** outer electrons · diatomic: Cl₂, Br₂, I₂ >> The halogens have seven outer electrons. They exist as pairs of atoms, called diatomic molecules.

@scene CHEMISTRY | Groups | Why reactivity decreases down Group 7
- Halogens react by **gaining** an electron >> Halogens react by gaining one electron.
- Going down: outer shell **further** from the nucleus >> Going down the group, the outer shell is further from the nucleus.
- → **harder** to attract an electron >> So it is harder to attract an extra electron.
- Reactivity **decreases** going down >> That's why reactivity decreases going down group seven. The opposite of group one!

@scene CHEMISTRY | Groups | Displacement reactions
@box Rule | more reactive halogen | pushes out | less reactive one
- A **more reactive** halogen displaces a less reactive one >> In a displacement reaction, a more reactive halogen pushes a less reactive one out of its salt solution.
- chlorine + potassium bromide → potassium chloride + bromine >> Chlorine plus potassium bromide gives potassium chloride plus bromine.
- Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂ >> Here is the balanced symbol equation.
- Solution turns **orange** (bromine) >> You see the solution turn orange, because bromine is made.
- Iodine + potassium chloride → **no reaction** >> Iodine plus potassium chloride: no reaction, because iodine is less reactive than chlorine.
- Halogen + metal → **metal halide** (a salt) >> Halogens also react with metals to make salts called metal halides, like sodium chloride.

@scene CHEMISTRY | Groups | Group 0: the noble gases
- **Full outer shell** → very unreactive (inert) >> The noble gases have a full outer shell, so they are very unreactive.
- Helium: balloons (low density, not flammable) >> Helium is used in balloons, because it is less dense than air and does not burn.
- Argon: light bulbs and welding (inert) >> Argon is used in light bulbs and welding, because it is inert, so it stops metal reacting with oxygen.
- Boiling point and density **increase** going down >> Going down the group, boiling point and density increase.

@chapter 4. Rates of reaction
@scene CHEMISTRY | Rates | Rate and collision theory
@diagram collision
- Rate = **how fast** a reaction happens >> The rate of reaction is how fast a reaction happens.
- Mean rate = amount of product ÷ time >> Mean rate is the amount of product made, divided by the time. Sixty centimetres cubed of gas in thirty seconds is two centimetres cubed per second.
- Particles must **collide**… >> For a reaction to happen, particles must collide,
- …with **enough energy** (activation energy) >> with enough energy. The minimum energy needed is the activation energy.
- Faster = **more frequent** collisions >> To speed up a reaction, we need more frequent collisions, or more collisions with enough energy.

@scene CHEMISTRY | Rates | Factors that change the rate
@box Exam phrase | "more frequent | collisions" | = more per second
- **Temperature** ↑: faster particles >> Higher temperature: particles move faster, so there are more frequent collisions, and more of them have enough energy.
- **Concentration** ↑: more particles in the same volume >> Higher concentration: more particles in the same volume, so more frequent collisions.
- **Pressure** ↑ (gases): same particles, smaller space >> Higher pressure, for gases: the same particles in a smaller space, so more frequent collisions.
- **Surface area** ↑: powder, not lumps >> Bigger surface area: a powder has more particles exposed than a lump, so more frequent collisions.
- **Catalyst**: lower activation energy, not used up >> A catalyst gives a route with a lower activation energy, and is not used up. Enzymes are biological catalysts.

@scene CHEMISTRY | Rates | Core practicals and graphs
@diagram rate_graph
- Marble chips + acid → measure **gas** or **mass lost** >> Edexcel uses two reactions. Marble chips with hydrochloric acid make carbon dioxide. Measure the volume of gas, or the mass lost.
- Change: **chip size** or acid concentration >> You can change the size of the chips, or the concentration of the acid.
- Sodium thiosulfate + acid → goes **cloudy** >> Sodium thiosulfate with hydrochloric acid goes cloudy. Time how long until the cross disappears.
- Change: **temperature** or concentration >> Here, you can change the temperature or the concentration.
- Steep = fast · flat = **finished** >> On the graph, steep means fast, and flat means the reaction has finished.

@chapter 5. Energy changes
@scene CHEMISTRY | Energy | Exothermic and endothermic
@box Remember | EXothermic = heat EXits | ENdothermic = heat ENters
- Exothermic: **gives out** heat → temperature rises >> An exothermic reaction gives out heat, so the temperature rises.
- Examples: **combustion**, **neutralisation** >> Examples are burning, and neutralisation, which is an acid plus an alkali.
- Endothermic: **takes in** heat → temperature falls >> An endothermic reaction takes in heat, so the temperature falls.
- Examples: thermal decomposition, dissolving **ammonium nitrate** >> Examples are thermal decomposition, and dissolving ammonium nitrate in water.
- Practical: **polystyrene cup** (insulator) >> In the practical, use a polystyrene cup with a lid. It is an insulator, so less heat is lost.

@scene CHEMISTRY | Energy | Bond energies
@box Energy change = | break − make | negative = exothermic
- **Breaking** bonds takes in energy (endothermic) >> Breaking bonds needs energy. That's endothermic.
- **Making** bonds gives out energy (exothermic) >> Making bonds releases energy. That's exothermic.
- H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl >> Let's calculate the energy change for hydrogen plus chlorine.
- Break: 436 + 242 = 678 >> Breaking: four hundred and thirty-six, plus two hundred and forty-two, is six hundred and seventy-eight.
- Make: 2 × 431 = 862 >> Making two HCl bonds: two times four hundred and thirty-one is eight hundred and sixty-two.
- 678 − 862 = **−184 kJ/mol** → exothermic >> Break minus make is minus one hundred and eighty-four kilojoules per mole. It's negative, so it's exothermic.

@scene CHEMISTRY | Energy | Reaction profiles
@diagram profile
- Exothermic: products **lower** than reactants >> On a reaction profile, an exothermic reaction has the products lower than the reactants. Energy was given out.
- Endothermic: products **higher** than reactants >> In an endothermic reaction, the products are higher.
- The "hump" = **activation energy** >> The hump, from the reactants up to the top, is the activation energy.
- A catalyst makes the hump **lower** >> A catalyst makes the hump lower.

@chapter 6. Fuels
@scene CHEMISTRY | Fuels | Crude oil
- Hydrocarbon = hydrogen + carbon **only** >> A hydrocarbon is a compound of hydrogen and carbon only.
- Crude oil: a complex **mixture** of hydrocarbons >> Crude oil is a complex mixture of hydrocarbons, mostly alkanes.
- **Finite**: non-renewable >> It is finite. It is being used up much faster than it forms.
- Source of fuels and **feedstock** for plastics >> It gives us fuels, and feedstock, which means raw materials for making plastics and other chemicals.

@scene CHEMISTRY | Fuels | Fractional distillation
@diagram column
- Heat oil until it **evaporates** >> In fractional distillation, the oil is heated until it evaporates.
- Column: hot at bottom, cool at top >> The gas rises up a column that is hot at the bottom and cool at the top.
- Fractions **condense** at their boiling points >> Each fraction condenses when it reaches its boiling point, and is collected.
- Petrol: cars · Kerosene: **aircraft** >> Petrol is used in cars. Kerosene is fuel for aircraft.
- Diesel: cars, trains · Fuel oil: **ships**, power stations >> Diesel oil is for cars and trains. Fuel oil is for ships and power stations.
- Gases: heating, cooking · Bitumen: **roads** >> Gases are for heating and cooking, and bitumen surfaces roads and roofs.

@scene CHEMISTRY | Fuels | Alkanes and the homologous series
@diagram ethane
- Bigger molecules: higher boiling point, thicker, harder to ignite >> Bigger molecules have higher boiling points, are thicker, and are harder to set alight.
- Homologous series: same **general formula** >> The alkanes are a homologous series. They have the same general formula,
- Each differs by **CH₂** >> each one differs from the next by CH₂,
- Similar chemical properties >> and they have similar chemical properties.
- Methane CH₄ · Ethane C₂H₆ · Propane C₃H₈ · Butane C₄H₁₀ >> The first four are methane, ethane, propane and butane.

@scene CHEMISTRY | Fuels | Burning fuels and pollution
- Complete: hydrocarbon + oxygen → CO₂ + H₂O >> Complete combustion, with plenty of oxygen, makes carbon dioxide and water.
- Incomplete: **carbon monoxide** + **soot** >> Incomplete combustion, without enough oxygen, also makes carbon monoxide and soot.
- CO: toxic, binds to **haemoglobin** >> Carbon monoxide is toxic. It stops your blood carrying oxygen. You can't see it or smell it.
- SO₂: from **sulfur impurities** → acid rain >> Sulfur impurities in fuels burn to make sulfur dioxide, which causes acid rain.
- NOₓ: N₂ + O₂ react in **hot engines** → acid rain >> In hot engines, nitrogen and oxygen from the air react to make nitrogen oxides. These cause acid rain too.

@scene CHEMISTRY | Fuels | Hydrogen as a fuel
@box Hydrogen | + only product: water | − hard to store | − explosive
- Only product: **water** (clean) >> Hydrogen is a clean fuel. When it burns, the only product is water.
- Hard to **store** (it's a gas) >> But it's a gas, so it's hard to store.
- **Explosive** >> It's explosive,
- Often made using fossil fuel energy >> and making it often uses energy from fossil fuels.

@scene CHEMISTRY | Fuels | Cracking
@diagram ethene
- Too many **long** chains, not enough short ones >> Crude oil has too many long chains, and not enough short ones like petrol.
- Cracking: break large alkanes into **smaller** molecules >> Cracking breaks large alkanes into smaller, more useful molecules.
- Heat + **hot catalyst** >> The vapour is heated and passed over a hot catalyst.
- C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄ >> For example, decane cracks into octane and ethene.
- Makes **alkenes** (C=C double bond) >> Cracking makes alkenes, which have a carbon to carbon double bond. They're used to make plastics.

@chapter 7. The atmosphere
@scene CHEMISTRY | Atmosphere | How the atmosphere changed
- Volcanoes: mostly **CO₂**, little or no oxygen >> The early atmosphere came from volcanoes. It was mostly carbon dioxide, with water vapour, and little or no oxygen.
- Earth cooled → water condensed → **oceans** >> As the Earth cooled, water vapour condensed to form the oceans.
- CO₂ ↓: dissolved in oceans, rocks, fossil fuels >> Carbon dioxide went down. It dissolved in the oceans, and was locked in rocks and fossil fuels.
- O₂ ↑: **photosynthesis** by algae and plants >> Oxygen went up because of photosynthesis by algae and plants.
- Test for oxygen: glowing splint **relights** >> The test for oxygen: a glowing splint relights.
- Today: 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen >> Today, the air is about seventy-eight percent nitrogen and twenty-one percent oxygen.

@scene CHEMISTRY | Atmosphere | The greenhouse effect
@diagram greenhouse
- Greenhouse gases: CO₂, methane, water vapour >> The greenhouse gases are carbon dioxide, methane and water vapour.
- Short-wavelength sunlight passes through >> Short wavelength radiation from the Sun passes through the atmosphere.
- Earth gives off **infrared** >> The warm Earth gives off longer wavelength infrared radiation.
- Gases **absorb** and **re-emit** it → Earth warms >> Greenhouse gases absorb this and send some back, keeping the Earth warm.
- Humans: fossil fuels, deforestation, cattle >> Humans add more by burning fossil fuels, cutting down forests, and farming cattle.

@scene CHEMISTRY | Atmosphere | Climate change
- CO₂ and temperature have **both risen** >> Evidence shows carbon dioxide levels and global temperatures have both risen together.
- Old data is **less accurate** >> But older data is less accurate. There were fewer measurements, from fewer places.
- Effects: rising sea levels, **flooding** >> Effects of climate change include rising sea levels and flooding,
- Extreme weather, food problems >> more extreme weather, and problems growing food.
- Reduce: renewables, **carbon capture** >> We can reduce it with renewable energy, carbon capture, and using less energy.
