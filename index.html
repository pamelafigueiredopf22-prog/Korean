한글 Hangul

The Korean alphabet. Tap any letter to hear it.

Korean isn't drawn as a line of letters — sounds stack into little square syllable blocks. A block is a consonant + a vowel: ㄱ + ㅏ = 가 (ga). Learn the pieces and you can read the blocks.

Vowels · 모음

These set the main sound of a block.

Combined vowels · 겹모음

Basic vowels run together into one sound. A few have nearly merged in modern Korean — ㅐ and ㅔ are both about e, and ㅙ ㅚ ㅞ all land near we.

Consonants · 자음

These start (and sometimes end) a block.

Tense consonants · 쌍자음

The same shapes doubled. Say them harder and tighter, with no puff of air — almost like you catch the sound before it leaves.

ㅇ is the quiet one. At the start of a block it makes no sound — it's just a placeholder so a vowel can stand alone: 아 is simply a. At the end of a block it sounds like ng.

발음 Pronunciation

Why Korean words aren’t said the way they’re spelled. Tap any example to hear it.

Korean spelling keeps each word looking the same, but speech runs syllables together, and sounds bend to fit their neighbours. These aren’t sloppiness or slang — they’re the standard rules (표준 발음법), and they’re what stands between knowing Hangul and actually reading Korean aloud.

Don’t memorise these. Read them once, then drill — they sink in through your ear, not your notes.
연음Linking

The big one. A final consonant (받침) can’t sit still if the next block starts with the silent ㅇ — it slides over into that empty slot.

받침The seven final sounds

Sixteen consonants can be written at the bottom of a block, but only seven sounds can actually close one: ㄱ ㄴ ㄷ ㄹ ㅁ ㅂ ㅇ. Everything else collapses into the nearest of those — ㅋ ㄲ → [ㄱ], ㅍ → [ㅂ], and ㄷ ㅌ ㅅ ㅆ ㅈ ㅊ ㅎ all → [ㄷ]. The sound also stops dead, with no puff of air after it.

비음화Nasalising

ㄴ and ㅁ are nasal, and they’re contagious. A hard stop landing in front of one turns nasal too: [ㄱ] → ㅇ, [ㄷ] → ㄴ, [ㅂ] → ㅁ.

유음화Liquidising

When ㄴ and ㄹ end up next to each other — in either order — the ㄴ gives in and becomes ㄹ.

경음화Tensing

After a [ㄱ] [ㄷ] [ㅂ] sound, a following ㄱ ㄷ ㅂ ㅅ ㅈ goes tense — ㄲ ㄸ ㅃ ㅆ ㅉ. Nothing is written differently; you just hear it tighten.

격음화Aspirating

ㅎ next to ㄱ ㄷ ㅂ ㅈ — before or after — merges with it and makes it breathy: ㅋ ㅌ ㅍ ㅊ.

ㅎ 탈락ㅎ dropping

The flip side: a ㅎ 받침 followed by a vowel just disappears. This one catches everybody — 좋아요 is never “조하요”.

구개음화Palatalising

ㄷ and ㅌ followed by 이 slide forward into ㅈ and ㅊ — the same laziness that turns English “would you” into “wouldja”.

겹받침Double batchim

Some blocks carry two consonants underneath (ㄺ ㅄ ㄵ ㄶ). Before a consonant only one of them sounds — but before a vowel, the second one links across instead: 읽다 [익따] yet 읽어요 [일거요].

조사 Particles

Little tags that tell you what job each word is doing.

English uses word order to show who did what to whom: “the cat drinks water” means something different from “the water drinks cat”. Korean tags each word instead — so the tag, not the position, carries the meaning. Three pairs matter first.

은 / 는Topic

Sets up what the sentence is about — “as for X…”. Use it to introduce yourself or your subject, and to contrast one thing with another.

저는 학생이에요.
As for me, I’m a student.
이 / 가Subject

Marks who or what is actually doing the verb, or being described. It tends to point something out as new or specific — “it’s the cat that’s cute”.

고양이가 귀여워요.
The cat is cute.
을 / 를Object

Marks what the verb is done to. This one is the least ambiguous of the three — if something is being drunk, eaten, read or drawn, it takes 을/를.

커피를 마셔요.
I drink coffee.

Then: which of the pair?

Once you know the job, the form is a spelling rule, not a judgement call. Look at the last letter of the noun:

ends in a consonant → 은 · 이 · 을
ends in a vowel → 는 · 가 · 를
It’s purely for the mouth: 이름은 is easier to say than 이름는.

The honest bit

Topic vs subject (은/는 vs 이/가) is the one that takes ages, and native speakers choose by nuance rather than rule — both are often grammatical in the same sentence. Don’t expect to nail it from a chart; you’ll absorb it from reading. That’s why every card in the drill tells you the role — so you can practise the ending rule cleanly, without guessing the nuance too.

문장 Sentence structure

Korean word order — and why the verb waits until the end.

English runs Subject → Verb → Object: “I drink coffee.” Korean runs Subject → Object → Verb — the verb always comes last.

저는 커피를 마셔요.
I · coffee · drink

The particles you already know (은/는, 이/가, 을/를) tag each word’s job, so Korean can reshuffle for emphasis. But the neutral, safe order is S–O–V, and the verb stays at the end no matter what.

과거 Past tense

A diary is mostly “today I did…”. Here’s how to say it.

Drop 다 from the dictionary form, look at the stem’s last vowel, and add one of three endings:

stem vowel ㅏ or ㅗ → 았어요
any other vowel → 었어요
하다 verbs → 했어요

Often the stem and ending squeeze together: 가 + 았어요 → 갔어요, 보 + 았어요 → 봤어요, 마시 + 었어요 → 마셨어요.

시제 Present & future

What you do, and what you’re going to do.

현재Present

Good news — it’s the same vowel rule you already learned for the past, just without the 았/었 in the middle. Drop 다, look at the stem’s last vowel:

stem vowel ㅏ or ㅗ → 아요
any other vowel → 어요
하다 verbs → 해요

This form covers both “I drink coffee” and “I’m drinking coffee” — Korean doesn’t split those. It also handles habits: 항상 커피를 마셔요.

미래Future

One pattern: -(으)ㄹ 거예요. Which half you use depends on the stem’s last sound — the same consonant-vs-vowel split as particles.

stem ends in a vowel → -ㄹ 거예요  가 → 갈 거예요
stem ends in a consonant → -을 거예요  먹 → 먹을 거예요

Three things worth getting right from the start:

  • It’s spelled 거예요, never 거에요 — even Koreans slip on this one.
  • There’s a space before it: 갈 거예요, not 갈거예요.
  • You’ll hear [갈 꺼예요] — that’s the tensing rule from Pronunciation. Don’t write the ㄲ.
All three tenses
어제 커피를 마셨어요.
오늘 커피를 마셔요.
내일 커피를 마실 거예요.
Yesterday I drank coffee. Today I drink coffee. Tomorrow I’ll drink coffee.

존댓말 Politeness

Korean doesn’t add “please”. It changes the end of the verb.

Everything you’ve learned in this app so far — 마셔요, 갔어요, 할 거예요 — is one particular level called 해요체. You already speak it. This unit shows you its neighbours.

Which level you use isn’t about how you feel; it’s about who’s listening. Age, rank, closeness and setting decide it. There are seven levels in the grammar books, but three carry modern life:

합니다체Formal

Presentations, job interviews, the news, the military, announcements, customer service. Crisp and public. You’ll hear far more of it than you produce.

해요체Polite

The everyday workhorse — shops, colleagues, new acquaintances, anyone you’re unsure about. Your default. It’s never offensive to be too polite.

반말Casual

Close friends of similar age, younger siblings, partners, talking to yourself. Mostly just 해요체 with the 요 dropped.

The part learners get wrong: you don’t decide to use 반말 on your own. The relationship decides, and the switch is usually offered out loud — 말 놓으세요 (“speak comfortably”). Using it uninvited with someone older or new reads as disrespect.

The same verb, three ways

Building the formal ending

It’s the same consonant-or-vowel question you’ve answered for particles and the future tense:

stem ends in a consonant → -습니다  먹 → 먹습니다
stem ends in a vowel → -ㅂ니다  가 → 갑니다
Questions swap 다 for 까: 갑니까? 먹습니까?

Notice you’ve already been saying this: 감사합니다 and 입니다 are 합니다체. And both follow your nasalising rule — [감사함니다], [임니다]. The ㅂ never sounds like a ㅂ here.

One exception worth parking: stems ending in ㄹ drop it before ㅂ니다 — 살다 → 삽니다, 만들다 → 만듭니다.

사람 People & pronouns

How to say I and we — and why “you” barely exists.

Korean has pronouns, but uses them far less than English does. If who you mean is obvious, you leave it out. 어디 가요? is a whole, natural sentence with no word for “you” anywhere in it.

저 · 나I, we

Two sets, split by politeness rather than grammar. 저 and 저희 are the humble forms for anyone you’d speak politely to; 나 and 우리 are for 반말. Korean shows respect by lowering yourself, not by raising the other person.

Two things to watch. The subject forms contract irregularly — 저 + 가 → 제가, never 저가. And 우리나라 stays 우리 even in the politest speech: you can’t humble a country you share with your listener.

One quirk with no English parallel: 우리 often covers what English calls my. 우리 엄마 is “my mum”, 우리 집 is “my house”.

당신?The “you” trap

Phrasebooks list 당신 as “you”. Treat that as a warning. In real speech it means one of three things: what spouses call each other, the “you” of adverts, songs and prayers — or, most often, the “you” of someone starting a fight.

당신 뭐야?
Not “what are you?” so much as “who do you think you are?” — this is a line from an argument.

너 isn’t a neutral substitute either: it’s intimate and downward, 반말 only. (Its subject form is 네가, though almost everyone says 니가 aloud to keep it distinct from 내가, “I”.)

Two details on 씨: attach it to a given name or full name, never a family name on its own (김 씨 is for someone beneath you), and it’s for peers and juniors — calling your boss 민수 씨 would land badly. For seniors, use their title with 님.

그분He, she, they

Strictly, Korean has no third-person pronouns. 그, 그녀 and 그들 exist, but they were invented by twentieth-century writers to translate European novels, and they still live on the page. Say them aloud and you sound like a book.

In speech: 그 사람 (that person), 그분 (the same, respectful), 그분들 for several. Or, most often, the person’s name — or nothing at all.

The habit to build
Each time you reach for “you”, try in this order: leave it out → their name + 씨 → their title. A bare pronoun is the last resort, not the first.

조사 2 Everyday particles

Three the app has leaned on without ever naming them.

에to · at

Marks a destination or a point in time. 학교에 가요 (to school), 아침에 (in the morning), 세 시에 (at three).

에서where it happens

When an action takes place somewhere, use 에서, not 에. 도서관에서 공부해요 (study at the library). The rule of thumb: 에 for going to a place or being at it; 에서 for doing something there.

있다 · 없다there is · isn't

있어요 = exists / I have. 없어요 = doesn't exist / I don't have. They're a pair of opposite words — 없다 is not 안 있다. With 에: 방에 고양이가 있어요.

하고and · with

Joins two nouns (빵하고 우유 = bread and milk) and means “with” for company (친구하고 = with a friend). Sticks straight onto the noun.

질문 Question words

The words that let you actually ask, not just answer.

Good news on placement: a question word usually just sits where the answer would go, right before the verb, and the sentence order doesn't change. 밥을 먹어요 → 뭐 먹어요? You don't invert anything the way English does.

뭐 · 어디 · 언제 · 왜 · 어떻게the core five
뭐 what · 뭐 먹어요? — what are you eating?
어디 where · 화장실이 어디예요?
언제 when · 언제 만날까요?
왜 why · 왜 안 왔어요?
어떻게 how · 어떻게 가요?

뭐 is the everyday form of the more formal 무엇. Any of these can stand alone as a one-word question too: 왜? 어디?

누구 → 누가who

Plain 누구 is “who” — 저 사람은 누구예요? But when “who” is the one doing the verb (the subject), it contracts to 누가: 누가 왔어요? Never 누구가.

몇 · 얼마how many · how much

몇 = how many / what number, and it clips onto a counter: 몇 시 (what time), 몇 개 (how many). 얼마 = how much it costs: 이거 얼마예요? Don't mix them — 몇 counts, 얼마 prices.

무슨what kind of

무슨 goes in front of a noun to ask “what kind of”: 무슨 음식 좋아해요? Where 뭐 stands alone, 무슨 leans on the noun after it.

표현 Want · please · must · can't

Four patterns that turn “I understand” into “I can actually do this”.

-고 싶어요want to

The easy one — it clips straight onto the stem, no vowel harmony. 가다 → 가고 싶어요, 먹다 → 먹고 싶어요, 마시다 → 마시고 싶어요.

한국에 가고 싶어요. — I want to go to Korea.
-아/어 주세요please (do for me)

Your single most useful travel pattern. Take the verb into its 아/어/해 form (the same harmony as the present tense) and add 주세요. 읽다 → 읽어 주세요, 보다 → 봐 주세요, 설명하다 → 설명해 주세요.

물 주세요. · 도와주세요. — Water please. / Help me.
-아/어야 해요have to / must

Same 아/어/해 form, then 야 해요. 가다 → 가야 해요, 먹다 → 먹어야 해요, 공부하다 → 공부해야 해요.

지금 가야 해요. — I have to go now.
못can't (unable)

Drop 못 right before the verb — it's the “can't” to match the 안 you learned for “don't”. 못 가요 (can't go), 못 먹어요 (can't eat).

Same 하다 split as 안: 공부 못 해요, never ✗못 공부해요.

안 = choose not to. 못 = unable to. 학교에 안 가요 (I'm not going) vs 학교에 못 가요 (I can't go).

부정 Negation

How to say you don't, didn't, and isn't.

Two ways to negate almost anything — same meaning, different register:

안short · casual

Drop 안 right before the verb: 안 가요, 안 먹었어요. This is what you'll say most of the time.

One catch: with 하다 verbs, 안 goes inside — 공부 안 해요, not ✗안 공부해요.
-지 않다long · careful

Attach -지 않다 to the stem: 가지 않아요, 공부하지 않아요. Sounds more formal and considered; common in writing. Works on every verb and adjective, no splitting.

special formsown word

A few don't take 안 at all — they flip to a different word:

있어요 (have) → 없어요
재미있어요 (fun) → 재미없어요
알아요 (know) → 몰라요

Aside: 안 is “don't (choose to)”. “Can't” is a different word, 못 — a lesson for later.

숫자 Numbers

Korean has two full number systems. The trick is knowing which.

고유어Native

하나, 둘, 셋, 넷, 다섯, 여섯, 일곱, 여덟, 아홉, 열. For counting things and people, your age, and the hour. Runs out at 99.

1–4 shrink before a counter: 하나→한, 둘→두, 셋→세, 넷→네. So “three apples” is 사과 세 개, not ✗셋 개.
한자어Sino

일, 이, 삼, 사, 오, 육, 칠, 팔, 구, 십. For money, dates, minutes, phone numbers, addresses — and anything big. Just 십(10), 백(100), 천(1,000), 만(10,000) and you can build the rest.

시 · 분the clock uses both

The famous one: the hour is native, the minute is Sino, in the same breath.

세 시 삼십 분
세 (native 3) 시 · 삼십 (Sino 30) 분 — 3:30

연결 Joining sentences

Turn two short sentences into one — the leap from choppy to flowing.

So far you've joined ideas with 그리고 between two full sentences. These endings do it inside one, attached to the first verb's stem.

-고and

Lists actions in sequence. 커피를 마시고 책을 읽어요 (drink coffee and read). Straight on the stem — 마시고, never ✗마셔고 or ✗마셨고.

-아서 / 어서so · because

First clause is the reason for the second. 피곤해서 잤어요 (tired, so slept). Uses the same 아/어 vowel rule as the present tense.

-지만but

Contrast. 싸지만 좋아요 (cheap but good). Straight on the stem.

The rule that catches everyone: tense goes on the last verb only. “I ate and drank” is 먹고 마셨어요 — the past sits on 마셨어요 alone, never on 먹고.
