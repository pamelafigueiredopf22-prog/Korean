## 3. `what` (0:45)
*Visual: Clance & Imes 1978. Not a diagnosis. The three beliefs. Bravata 2020: 9 to 82 percent.*

1. In 1978, psychologists Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes were working with very successful women. Women with top grades, degrees and awards.  
   ↳ `voice/what_1.wav`
2. And they noticed something strange. Many of these women were convinced that they weren't actually intelligent, and that they had fooled everyone around them. Clance and Imes called it the impostor phenomenon. At first, they thought it mainly affected women. But later research found it in men too, often at similar rates.  
   ↳ `voice/what_2.wav`
3. It isn't an official mental health diagnosis. It's a pattern of thinking, built around three beliefs. I'm not as capable as people think. My success came from luck, or charm, or someone's mistake. And I'm about to be exposed.  
   ↳ `voice/what_3.wav`
4. Clance later created a questionnaire to measure it, with statements like: I can give the impression that I'm more competent than I really am. And: I'm afraid people important to me may find out that I'm not as capable as they think.  
   ↳ `voice/what_4.wav`
5. And it's very common. In a 2020 review of sixty two studies, led by Dena Bravata, the rates varied enormously, from about nine percent to eighty two percent, depending on who was studied and how it was measured. It showed up in men and women, students and professionals, across many different fields.  
   ↳ `voice/what_5.wav`
6. Even very famous people describe it. The writer Maya Angelou, who wrote eleven books, is often quoted as saying that each time, she thought: uh oh, they're going to find out now.  
   ↳ `voice/what_6.wav`

## 4. `cycle` (2:19)
*Visual: Clance's cycle: task -> anxiety -> over-prepare or procrastinate -> success -> 'luck / effort' -> doubt returns.*

1. Pauline Clance later described something called the impostor cycle. And once you see it, it's hard to unsee.  
   ↳ `voice/cycle_1.wav`
2. It starts with a new task. A presentation, an exam, a project. And with it, a wave of anxiety and self-doubt.  
   ↳ `voice/cycle_2.wav`
3. Then, people usually react in one of two ways. Some over-prepare, working far more than they need to. Others procrastinate, and then rush at the last minute.  
   ↳ `voice/cycle_3.wav`
4. The task goes well. But instead of feeling proud, the person explains it away. If they over-prepared, they think: I only succeeded because I worked so hard, not because I'm good. If they procrastinated: I just got lucky this time.  
   ↳ `voice/cycle_4.wav`
5. Either way, the success doesn't count. So the next time a challenge comes along, the doubt is right back. And the cycle starts again. And it can keep repeating for years, no matter how much a person actually achieves.  
   ↳ `voice/cycle_5.wav`

## 5. `why` (3:15)
*Visual: Hiding the struggle (pluralistic ignorance). Seeing others' highlight reels. Being 'the only one' in a room. Experts underestimate themselves.*

1. So why do so many capable people feel like this? Researchers point to a few ingredients.  
   ↳ `voice/why_1.wav`
2. One. We see our own messy insides, but only other people's polished outsides. You know every moment of panic you've had, but you rarely see your colleagues' doubts. So it's easy to conclude that everyone else has it figured out. And social media can make it even worse, with an endless stream of other people's highlights.  
   ↳ `voice/why_2.wav`
3. Psychologists call this pluralistic ignorance: when lots of people privately feel the same way, but each one assumes they're the only one.  
   ↳ `voice/why_3.wav`
4. Two. Being different from the people around you. Researchers have suggested that being the first in your family to go to university, or one of very few people like you in your field, can make it easier to feel like you don't belong.  
   ↳ `voice/why_4.wav`
5. Three. The more you know, the more you know what you don't know. In the original study by David Dunning and Justin Kruger, the best performers actually tended to underestimate how well they'd done compared to others. Real expertise often comes with real awareness of your own gaps.  
   ↳ `voice/why_5.wav`
6. Four. Family labels. In their original paper, Clance and Imes noticed that some of the women had grown up being seen as the sensitive one, while a sibling was the smart one. Others had been told they were brilliant at everything, so any struggle felt like proof that they weren't. Either way, the label stuck.  
   ↳ `voice/why_6.wav`
7. Five. Perfectionism. Studies have found strong links between impostor feelings and perfectionism. If anything less than flawless feels like failure, then almost everything starts to look like evidence that you're a fraud.  
   ↳ `voice/why_7.wav`

## 6. `cost` (4:56)
*Visual: Bravata: linked with anxiety, depression, burnout. Tewfik 2022: impostor thoughts and interpersonal effectiveness.*

1. Impostor feelings aren't just uncomfortable. In Bravata's review, they were often linked with anxiety, depression and burnout. People may also turn down opportunities, avoid asking for help, or never apply for the promotion at all.  
   ↳ `voice/cost_1.wav`
2. But here's a surprising twist. In research by Basima Tewfik at MIT, employees who had impostor thoughts more often were actually rated by others as better with people. More likable, more collaborative.  
   ↳ `voice/cost_2.wav`
3. Tewfik's explanation is that people who worry about not being good enough may put more effort into listening and connecting with others.  
   ↳ `voice/cost_3.wav`
4. So feeling like an impostor doesn't mean you are one. Sometimes, it's a sign that you care about doing things well.  
   ↳ `voice/cost_4.wav`

## 7. `tips` (5:43)
*Visual: Six ideas: name it, talk about it, keep an evidence file, separate feelings from facts, 'learner not fraud', get support. Research on treatments is limited.*

1. So what can you do about it? To be honest, there isn't much research yet on treatments specifically for impostor feelings. But here are some ideas that psychologists commonly recommend, and that are grounded in what we know.  
   ↳ `voice/tips_1.wav`
2. One. Name it. When the voice says you're a fraud, notice it and label it: there's the impostor feeling again. Just naming a feeling can make it a little less powerful.  
   ↳ `voice/tips_2.wav`
3. Two. Talk about it. Remember pluralistic ignorance. When you share these feelings with a friend or a colleague you trust, there's a good chance they'll say: wait, you feel that too?  
   ↳ `voice/tips_3.wav`
4. Three. Keep an evidence file. Save the kind emails, the good feedback, the problems you solved. When your feelings say you've achieved nothing, your evidence file can say otherwise. Read it before big moments, like a presentation or an interview.  
   ↳ `voice/tips_4.wav`
5. Four. Separate feelings from facts. Feeling like you don't belong is a real feeling, but it isn't proof. Ask yourself: what would I say about a friend with my exact track record?  
   ↳ `voice/tips_5.wav`
6. Five. Swap fraud for learner. Nobody knows everything in a new role. Not knowing something yet doesn't mean you shouldn't be there. It means you're learning.  
   ↳ `voice/tips_6.wav`
7. Six. Find a mentor, or someone a few steps ahead of you. Hearing that they felt the same way when they started can be incredibly reassuring.  
   ↳ `voice/tips_7.wav`
8. And seven. If these feelings are constant and come with anxiety, low mood or exhaustion, talking to a therapist can really help. Approaches like cognitive behavioral therapy are designed to work with exactly these kinds of thoughts.  
   ↳ `voice/tips_8.wav`
