## 3. `rem` (0:52)
*Visual: 1953 Aserinsky & Kleitman. Sleep cycles. REM ~ every 90 minutes. Dreams in non-REM too.*

1. In 1953, researchers Eugene Aserinsky and Nathaniel Kleitman noticed something odd about sleeping people. Every so often, their eyes darted back and forth beneath their eyelids.  
   ↳ `voice/rem_1.wav`
2. They called it rapid eye movement sleep, or REM sleep. And when they woke people up during REM, most of them said they'd been dreaming.  
   ↳ `voice/rem_2.wav`
3. We now know that sleep comes in cycles, of roughly ninety minutes. Each cycle moves from light sleep, into deep sleep, and then into REM. And the REM periods get longer toward the morning. Newborn babies spend around half of their sleep in REM. For adults, it's closer to a fifth or a quarter.  
   ↳ `voice/rem_3.wav`
4. During REM, your brain is surprisingly active, almost as active as when you're awake. But your body's major muscles are temporarily paralyzed, which probably stops you from acting out your dreams.  
   ↳ `voice/rem_4.wav`
5. For a long time, people thought dreams only happened in REM. But later research found that people dream in other stages too. Those dreams just tend to be shorter, and less wild.  
   ↳ `voice/rem_5.wav`

## 4. `themes` (2:01)
*Visual: Typical dreams: chased, falling, school. Negative emotions common. Continuity with waking life.*

1. So what do we actually dream about? Surprisingly, people all over the world report similar themes.  
   ↳ `voice/themes_1.wav`
2. In a survey by Tore Nielsen and his colleagues, the most common dream was being chased. Around eight in ten people said they'd had it at least once. Falling was close behind, and so were dreams about school, teachers and exams. Just like Sam's.  
   ↳ `voice/themes_2.wav`
3. Dreams also tend to be more negative than you might expect. When researchers have analyzed thousands of dream reports, emotions like fear, anxiety and frustration show up more often than happiness.  
   ↳ `voice/themes_3.wav`
4. But dreams aren't completely random either. They often borrow people, places and worries from our daily lives, remixed in very strange ways. Researchers call this the continuity hypothesis.  
   ↳ `voice/themes_4.wav`
5. And here's something remarkable. People who have been blind since birth dream too. Their dreams just aren't built from images, but from sounds, touch, smells and tastes.

## 5. `theories` (3:03)
*Visual: Freud's wish fulfillment. Activation-synthesis. Threat simulation. Memory and emotion processing: Wamsley maze study, rat replay.*

1. So why do we dream? Here are the four big ideas.  
   ↳ `voice/theories_1.wav`
2. Idea one comes from Sigmund Freud, over a hundred years ago. He believed dreams were hidden wishes in disguise. It's a famous theory, but modern research has found very little evidence to support it.  
   ↳ `voice/theories_2.wav`
3. Idea two, from the nineteen seventies, is called activation-synthesis. Psychiatrists Allan Hobson and Robert McCarley suggested that during REM, the brainstem fires off fairly random signals, and the rest of the brain tries its best to stitch them into a story.  
   ↳ `voice/theories_3.wav`
4. Idea three is threat simulation. The Finnish researcher Antti Revonsuo argued that dreams may be a kind of rehearsal, a safe place to practice escaping danger. That could explain why being chased is so common.  
   ↳ `voice/theories_4.wav`
5. And idea four, which has a lot of support today: dreams may be a side effect of the brain processing memories and emotions while we sleep. Some researchers, like Matthew Walker, have suggested that REM sleep may even help take the emotional sting out of difficult memories, a kind of overnight therapy. That idea is still being tested.  
   ↳ `voice/theories_5.wav`
6. In one study by Erin Wamsley, Robert Stickgold and their colleagues, people learned to find their way through a virtual maze, and then took a nap. Those who dreamed about the maze improved far more when they tried again afterwards.  
   ↳ `voice/theories_6.wav`
7. And it's not just humans. When researchers recorded the brains of rats who'd run through a maze, the same patterns of brain activity replayed during their sleep, as if they were running it again.  
   ↳ `voice/theories_7.wav`

## 6. `forget` (4:43)
*Visual: Dreams fade within minutes. Memory-making chemistry is different in sleep. Writing them down helps.*

1. If dreams are so important, why do we forget most of them?  
   ↳ `voice/forget_1.wav`
2. Part of the reason seems to be that the brain's memory-saving system works differently during sleep. Some of the chemicals that help us store new memories are much lower during REM.  
   ↳ `voice/forget_2.wav`
3. So unless you wake up during or right after a dream, and think about it, it usually just fades away. Some people simply remember more dreams than others, and research suggests they tend to wake up briefly more often during the night.  
   ↳ `voice/forget_3.wav`
4. If you want to remember more of your dreams, the best trick is simple. Keep a notebook by your bed, and write down whatever you can remember the moment you wake up, before you even check your phone.  
   ↳ `voice/forget_4.wav`

## 7. `lucid` (5:25)
*Visual: Lucid dreaming ~55%. Konkoly 2021 two-way communication. Sleep paralysis ~8%.*

1. Now for the strangest part. Have you ever realized you were dreaming, while you were still in the dream?  
   ↳ `voice/lucid_1.wav`
2. That's called a lucid dream. A review of studies found that around half of people have had at least one, and about a quarter have them regularly.  
   ↳ `voice/lucid_2.wav`
3. And in 2021, a team led by Karen Konkoly and Ken Paller did something incredible. They actually communicated with people while they were lucid dreaming. The researchers asked simple math questions, and the dreamers answered by moving their eyes in a pre-agreed pattern, while still asleep.  
   ↳ `voice/lucid_3.wav`
4. There's also a scarier experience: sleep paralysis. You wake up, but your body is still in REM mode, so you can't move, and sometimes you feel like someone is in the room. It's frightening, but it's usually harmless, and it happens to roughly eight percent of people at some point.  
   ↳ `voice/lucid_4.wav`

## 8. `night` (6:18)
*Visual: Sleep well, imagery rehearsal for nightmares, dream journal, when to get help.*

1. So, can you have better dreams? A few research-backed ideas.  
   ↳ `voice/night_1.wav`
2. One. Protect your sleep. Most REM sleep happens in the last hours of the night, so cutting your sleep short cuts your dreaming time short too. Alcohol before bed can also suppress REM sleep.  
   ↳ `voice/night_2.wav`
3. Two. If you have recurring nightmares, there's a technique called imagery rehearsal therapy. While you're awake, you rewrite the nightmare with a new, better ending, and rehearse it in your mind for a few minutes each day. Studies have found it can reduce how often nightmares happen.  
   ↳ `voice/night_3.wav`
4. Three. Keep a dream journal. It helps you remember more, and you might start to notice patterns, like stress at work showing up in your dreams as being chased.  
   ↳ `voice/night_4.wav`
5. And four. If nightmares are frequent, very distressing, or linked to a traumatic experience, talk to a doctor or a therapist. There are effective treatments.  
   ↳ `voice/night_5.wav`
