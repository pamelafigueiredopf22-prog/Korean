1. But embarrassment gets extra special treatment. Here's why.  
   ↳ `voice/tribe_1.wav`
2. For most of human history, people lived in small groups. Being accepted by the group meant food, protection, and survival. Being pushed out could be dangerous.  
   ↳ `voice/tribe_2.wav`
3. So our brains became very sensitive to anything that might hurt our place in the group. Embarrassment works like an alarm that says: careful, they might think less of you.  
   ↳ `voice/tribe_3.wav`
4. It helps to know that psychologists separate embarrassment from shame. Embarrassment is usually about a small, awkward slip in front of others. Shame is a heavier feeling about who you are. ## 9. `helps` (5:58)
*Visual: Four tips: fly on the wall, talk to yourself by name, what would you tell a friend, don't fight it (the white bear). Then: the cringe means you care.*

1. So, what can you actually do when the cringe attack hits?  
   ↳ `voice/helps_1.wav`
2. First, step back. Psychologists Ethan Kross and Ozlem Ayduk found that replaying a painful memory like a fly on the wall, watching yourself from the outside, tends to make it less upsetting.  
   ↳ `voice/helps_2.wav`
3. Second, try talking to yourself by name. Instead of, why did I do that, try: Sam, everyone says weird things sometimes. Research by Kross and his colleagues suggests that this small switch can make emotions easier to manage.  
   ↳ `voice/helps_3.wav`
4. Third, ask what you'd say to a friend. If a friend told you this story, you'd laugh with them, not at them. Psychologists call this self-compassion, and it's linked to less rumination.  
   ↳ `voice/helps_4.wav`
5. Another option: find the funny side. In experiments by Andrea Samson and James Gross, looking for a good-natured, light-hearted angle on upsetting images helped people feel less upset. Today's cringe can become tomorrow's best story.  
   ↳ `voice/helps_5.wav`
6. And don't try too hard to push the memory away. In a classic experiment by Daniel Wegner, people who were told not to think about a white bear ended up thinking about it more. Let the memory pass, like a car driving by.  
   ↳ `voice/helps_6.wav`
7. And if embarrassing memories are constant, very distressing, or stopping you from living your life, it's worth talking to a professional. Sometimes it's a sign of social anxiety or depression, and both are very treatable.  
   ↳ `voice/helps_7.wav`
8. Finally, remember what the cringe really means. It's your brain trying to protect your relationships. It means you care about other people. And that's not a flaw.
