## 3. `fresh` (0:37)
*Visual: Search and gym spikes at temporal landmarks. Old me vs new me.*

1. First, why do we even make resolutions in January?

## 4. `work` (1:24)
*Visual: Norcross: 46% vs 4%. Oscarsson: more than half successful after a year.*

1. You've probably heard that almost all resolutions fail. But when researchers actually tracked people, the picture was more hopeful.  
   ↳ `voice/work_1.wav`
2. In a study led by John Norcross, about forty six percent of people who made a resolution were still on track six months later. Among people who wanted to change but didn't make a resolution, it was only about four percent.  
   ↳ `voice/work_2.wav`
3. And in a larger Swedish study led by Martin Oscarsson, more than half of the participants considered themselves successful a full year later.  
   ↳ `voice/work_3.wav`
4. Interestingly, in Norcross's research, the people who succeeded tended to use practical strategies, like changing their surroundings and rewarding themselves, while the ones who struggled leaned more on wishful thinking and self-blame.  
   ↳ `voice/work_4.wav`
5. And decades of research on goal setting, led by Edwin Locke and Gary Latham, show that specific goals work better than vague ones. I'll get fitter is a wish. I'll walk for ten minutes after breakfast is a plan.  
   ↳ `voice/work_5.wav`
6. So resolutions can work. They just need a better design.  
   ↳ `voice/work_6.wav`

1. Let's start with a popular myth: that it takes twenty one days to form a habit.  
   ↳ `voice/myth_1.wav`
2. That number seems to trace back to a nineteen sixties self-help book, not to a scientific study.  
   ↳ `voice/myth_2.wav`
3. When the psychologist Phillippa Lally and her colleagues actually measured it, people took about sixty six days on average for a new daily behavior to feel automatic. And the range was huge: from eighteen days, to an estimated two hundred and fifty four. So if day twenty one comes and goes and it still feels like hard work, nothing is wrong with you. You're just not there yet.  
   ↳ `voice/myth_3.wav`
4. Simple habits, like drinking a glass of water with breakfast, became automatic faster than harder ones, like doing fifty sit-ups.  
   ↳ `voice/myth_4.wav`
5. And here's the most encouraging part. Missing a single day didn't really affect the process. One slip doesn't reset you to zero.

## 6. `traps` (3:20)
*Visual: Four traps: too many goals, the what-the-hell effect, avoidance goals, willpower alone.*

1. So why does Ivy quit by mid January? A few common traps.  
   ↳ `voice/traps_1.wav`
2. Trap one: too many goals at once, and too big. Running, no sugar, fifty books, Italian. Each one competes for the same limited time and attention.  
   ↳ `voice/traps_2.wav`
3. Psychologists Janet Polivy and Peter Herman even gave this a name: false hope syndrome. We expect fast, dramatic change, set goals that are far too ambitious, fall short, feel bad, and then try again next year with the same giant list.  
   ↳ `voice/traps_3.wav`
4. Trap two: the what the hell effect. In classic studies by Peter Herman and Janet Polivy, dieters who believed they'd already broken their diet often ate more, not less. One cookie becomes, well, what the hell, the whole box. Once the rule feels broken, it stops protecting you.  
   ↳ `voice/traps_4.wav`
5. Trap three: goals that are only about stopping things. In the Swedish study, people with approach goals, like eat more fruit, were more successful than people with avoidance goals, like stop eating sweets. About fifty nine percent, versus forty seven percent.  
   ↳ `voice/traps_5.wav`
6. And trap four: relying on willpower alone. Which brings us to the real secret.  
   ↳ `voice/traps_6.wav`

## 7. `cues` (4:32)
*Visual: 43% of daily actions are habits. Cue -> routine chains. Students moving to a new university; the TV in a new place.*

1. The psychologist Wendy Wood estimates that around forty three percent of what we do every day is done out of habit, often while we're thinking about something else.  
   ↳ `voice/cues_1.wav`
2. Habits run on cues. A place, a time, a previous action. Coffee machine, then phone. Couch, then TV.  
   ↳ `voice/cues_2.wav`
3. In one study by Wood and her colleagues, students who transferred to a new university found that some old habits changed, especially when the cues around them changed too. A TV watching habit, for example, was easier to break when the TV was in a different place.  
   ↳ `voice/cues_3.wav`
4. That's why it's so hard to change your behavior in the same old environment, and why a new place, or a new routine, can be a great moment to start.  
   ↳ `voice/cues_4.wav`
5. And good habits might be the secret behind people who seem to have amazing self-control. In a study by Brian Galla and Angela Duckworth, people with more self-control also tended to have stronger good habits, and those habits explained part of why they did better. They weren't just resisting temptation all day. Their good choices were on autopilot.  
   ↳ `voice/cues_5.wav`

## 8. `tips` (5:40)
*Visual: Six tips plus support.*

1. So here's how to give your resolution a fighting chance.  
   ↳ `voice/tips_1.wav`
2. One. Pick one goal, and make it small. Not, run every morning. Start with: put on your running shoes and walk for five minutes after breakfast.  
   ↳ `voice/tips_2.wav`
3. Two. Attach it to a cue. Use an if-then plan: after I pour my morning coffee, I'll stretch for two minutes. The routine you already have becomes the trigger. Psychologists call these implementation intentions, and a big review by Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran found that they reliably help people follow through.  
   ↳ `voice/tips_3.wav`
4. Three. Make it enjoyable now. In research by Kaitlin Woolley and Ayelet Fishbach, how much people enjoyed an activity in the moment predicted whether they stuck with it far better than the long-term benefits did. So pick the workout you actually like.  
   ↳ `voice/tips_4.wav`
5. Four. Change your environment. Put the fruit on the counter and the cookies out of sight. Leave your shoes by the door. Make the good choice the easy one.  
   ↳ `voice/tips_5.wav`
6. Five. Phrase it as something to do, not something to avoid. Eat more vegetables, rather than never eat junk food.  
   ↳ `voice/tips_6.wav`
7. Six. Track it. A large review led by Benjamin Harkin, combining over a hundred studies, found that monitoring your progress made people more likely to reach their goals, especially when they actually wrote it down. A simple calendar with checkmarks works.  
   ↳ `voice/tips_7.wav`
8. And seven. When you slip, and you will, don't throw it all away. Remember, one missed day doesn't reset the clock. Just start again tomorrow.  
   ↳ `voice/tips_8.wav`
9. One last thing. In the Swedish study, people who got a bit of support, like information and regular check-ins, did better than people who went it alone. So tell a friend.  
   ↳ `voice/tips_9.wav`
