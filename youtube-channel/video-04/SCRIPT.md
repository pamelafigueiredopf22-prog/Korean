## 3. `what` (0:43)
*Visual: Three overlapping circles. Sliders showing these are spectrums. A 'no armchair diagnosis' warning. The dark core, D.*

1. In 2002, the psychologists Delroy Paulhus and Kevin Williams noticed that three personality traits kept showing up together. They called them the Dark Triad.  
   ↳ `voice/what_1.wav`
2. Here's the first important thing. They were studying traits in everyday people, not people with a diagnosis. These are spectrums. Everyone sits somewhere on them, and most of us sit fairly low.  
   ↳ `voice/what_2.wav`
3. And the second thing. A high score doesn't make someone a monster. And you can't diagnose anyone from a YouTube video, including this one. Only trained professionals can do that.  
   ↳ `voice/what_3.wav`
4. So what do the three traits have in common? A team led by Morten Moshagen found that dark traits share a common core. They called it the dark factor, or D. The tendency to put your own interests first, even when it harms others, along with beliefs that justify it.  
   ↳ `voice/what_4.wav`

## 4. `nina` (1:36)
*Visual: Nina (pink, crown). The Narcissus myth. First impressions vs. seventh meeting. Grandiose vs vulnerable.*

1. Let's start with the dazzling one. Meet Nina.  
   ↳ `voice/nina_1.wav`
2. Narcissism is named after Narcissus, a figure from Greek mythology who fell in love with his own reflection. At its core, it's about grandiosity. Feeling special, superior, and entitled to admiration.  
   ↳ `voice/nina_2.wav`
3. And here's the fascinating part. Narcissists are often genuinely liked at first. In a study by Mitja Back and colleagues, students who scored higher in narcissism made better first impressions, partly thanks to their confident body language and stylish appearance.  
   ↳ `voice/nina_3.wav`
4. But it doesn't always last. In an earlier study by Delroy Paulhus, group members rated narcissists positively after their first meeting. By the seventh meeting, they saw them as more arrogant and less warm.  
   ↳ `voice/nina_4.wav`
5. Psychologists also describe a second, quieter type, called vulnerable narcissism. Instead of loud and confident, it looks more insecure, defensive, and easily hurt by criticism, but still very focused on the self.  
   ↳ `voice/nina_5.wav`
6. And remember, the everyday trait isn't the same as the clinical diagnosis. Narcissistic personality disorder is much rarer. The diagnostic manual puts it somewhere between zero and about six percent in community samples, depending on the study.  
   ↳ `voice/nina_6.wav`

## 5. `mack` (2:59)
*Visual: Mack (green, chess). Machiavelli and The Prince. The Mach questionnaire. Strategy: plays the long game.*

1. Next, the polite one who always knows what to say. Meet Mack.  
   ↳ `voice/mack_1.wav`
2. Machiavellianism is named after Niccolò Machiavelli, an Italian writer whose sixteenth century book, The Prince, gave rulers some famously ruthless advice. For example: it is much safer to be feared than loved.  
   ↳ `voice/mack_2.wav`
3. In the nineteen seventies, the psychologists Richard Christie and Florence Geis turned these ideas into a questionnaire. People who score high tend to have a cynical view of others, and to see relationships as tools.  
   ↳ `voice/mack_3.wav`
4. The key word is strategic. Someone high in Machiavellianism plays the long game. They're patient, calculating, and willing to flatter or deceive when it pays off.  
   ↳ `voice/mack_4.wav`
5. But here's a surprise. Despite their reputation as master manipulators, studies generally haven't found that people high in Machiavellianism are better at reading other people's emotions. Some studies even find they're a little worse.  
   ↳ `voice/mack_5.wav`

## 6. `pierce` (3:59)
*Visual: Pierce (grey, slick hair). Traits. Movies vs reality. Prevalence. The empathy study: a switch that's usually off.*

1. And finally, the fearless one. Meet Pierce.  
   ↳ `voice/pierce_1.wav`
2. Psychopathy, as a trait, combines boldness and thrill seeking with impulsiveness, and most importantly, a lack of empathy and remorse.  
   ↳ `voice/pierce_2.wav`
3. Movies make psychopaths look like serial killers. But many people with high psychopathic traits are not violent at all. Psychopathy also isn't an official diagnosis in the main psychiatric manual. The closest one is antisocial personality disorder.  
   ↳ `voice/pierce_3.wav`
4. How common is it? A 2021 review found that, using the strictest clinical tool, about one percent of adults would qualify. Broader questionnaires give higher estimates, around four to five percent. And in most studies, it's more common in men than in women.  
   ↳ `voice/pierce_4.wav`
5. And empathy is more complicated than it sounds. In a study by Harma Meffert and Christian Keysers, offenders with psychopathy showed weaker empathy-related brain activity while watching others. But when they were asked to try to feel with the people on screen, the difference shrank a lot.  
   ↳ `voice/pierce_5.wav`
6. So it may be less like an empathy switch that's broken, and more like one that's usually turned off.  
   ↳ `voice/pierce_6.wav`

1. So how do the three compare? Think about what drives each one.  
   ↳ `voice/compare_1.wav`
2. The narcissist wants admiration. They need an audience.  
   ↳ `voice/compare_2.wav`
3. The Machiavellian wants control and advantage, and plans carefully to get it.  
   ↳ `voice/compare_3.wav`
4. And the psychopath wants excitement and reward, right now, often without thinking about the consequences.  
   ↳ `voice/compare_4.wav`
5. That's a useful difference in timing. Machiavellianism tends to be long-term and strategic, while psychopathy is more short-term and impulsive.  
   ↳ `voice/compare_5.wav`
6. Where do these traits come from? In a twin study by Philip Vernon and colleagues, narcissism and psychopathy seemed to be strongly influenced by genes, while Machiavellianism seemed to depend more on environment and experience. Either way, genes are never the whole story.  
   ↳ `voice/compare_6.wav`
7. In 2013, researchers even suggested adding a fourth trait: everyday sadism, enjoying other people's pain. In a study by Erin Buckels and colleagues, participants could choose to put live bugs into a machine that looked like a grinder. The bugs were never actually harmed, but the people who chose that task scored higher in sadism.  
   ↳ `voice/compare_7.wav`

## 8. `real` (6:24)
*Visual: Workplace meta-analysis. How to protect yourself. Self-awareness.*

1. So do dark traits help people succeed? Sometimes they help people get noticed. But a large review of workplace studies by Ernest O'Boyle and colleagues found that all three traits were linked to more counterproductive work behavior. Things like cheating, bullying, or stealing.  
   ↳ `voice/real_1.wav`
2. So how do you protect yourself? A few general ideas. Watch what people do over time, not just what they say at the start. Notice when flattery comes with requests. And trust your own boundaries. You don't need to diagnose someone to step away from behavior that hurts you.  
   ↳ `voice/real_2.wav`
3. And if you're worried that you might have some of these traits, that worry is actually a sign of self-awareness. Everyone has a little darkness, and people can and do change how they behave.  
   ↳ `voice/real_3.wav`

## 9. `light` (7:11)
*Visual: The Light Triad: Kantianism, humanism, faith in humanity. Most people lean light.*

1. But let's end on some good news. In 2019, the psychologist Scott Barry Kaufman and his colleagues proposed the opposite: the Light Triad.  
   ↳ `voice/light_1.wav`
2. It's made of three traits. Kantianism: treating people as people, not as tools. Humanism: valuing the dignity of every person. And faith in humanity: believing that people are basically good.  
   ↳ `voice/light_2.wav`
3. And these traits aren't just nice to have. In Kaufman's research, people who scored higher on the Light Triad also tended to report more satisfaction with their lives.  
   ↳ `voice/light_3.wav`
4. And in their samples, most people leaned more toward the light side than the dark. Darkness gets the headlines. But kindness is surprisingly common.  
   ↳ `voice/light_4.wav`
